# Internal Digital Workplace for a 10,000-Employee Company

## Strategic Proposal for Useful Services on an Isolated Intranet

**Date:** 6 October 2026  
**Audience:** Management, department leaders, IT, and security stakeholders  
**Status:** Proposal for discussion and prioritization  
**Basis:** Company circumstances and service ideas discussed with the requester

---

## Executive Summary

The company has approximately 10,000 employees and an internal network without Internet access for security reasons. Employees therefore cannot use many public services that support information discovery, collaboration, learning, and business administration.

This report proposes an Internal Digital Workplace: a collection of company-operated intranet services accessible through a common employee portal. Its purpose is to help employees find information, locate colleagues, complete requests, coordinate work, and reuse organizational knowledge while keeping company information within its approved environment.

The proposal contains 40 service types. These are a portfolio of options, not a recommendation to build 40 separate applications immediately. Existing systems should be assessed first; suitable capabilities can be retained, connected, improved, or consolidated.

The recommended starting scope is an enterprise portal, employee directory, knowledge base, limited enterprise search, and a shared notification experience. Workflow, forms, service requests, and internal Q&A can follow. Document collaboration, learning, video, and internal AI should expand the portfolio when content quality, ownership, operational support, and permission controls are ready.

Management should approve an initial discovery and pilot phase, nominate service owners, and select a small number of measurable employee problems. Company-wide implementation should depend on pilot results and operational readiness.

## 1. Background and Business Problem

A large organization generates extensive information across departments, projects, documents, messages, and business applications. Employees may know that information exists but struggle to find its location, identify its owner, or determine whether it is current.

An isolated intranet also removes access to familiar Internet tools. The company can address this by reproducing useful business capabilities internally, with services designed around its actual procedures and access rules.

The following problems are hypotheses to validate during discovery:

| Potential problem | Employee impact | Relevant response |
|---|---|---|
| Many application addresses and inconsistent navigation | Time spent finding the right system | Portal and application catalog |
| Policies and procedures scattered across folders | Repeated questions and outdated guidance | Knowledge base and enterprise search |
| Expertise known only through personal contacts | Slow troubleshooting and weak knowledge sharing | Directory, Q&A, and expert finder |
| Requests managed through email and spreadsheets | Unclear ownership, status, and delays | Forms, workflow, and ticket services |
| Notifications spread across applications | Missed approvals and excessive interruptions | Notification center and personal work queue |
| Knowledge leaves with employees | Repeated work and difficult onboarding | Wiki, training, and documented answers |
| Shared resources booked informally | Conflicting reservations and poor visibility | Resource booking |

## 2. Objectives and Scope

### 2.1 Objectives

1. Provide one practical starting point for daily work.
2. Reduce the effort needed to find approved information and responsible people.
3. Make business requests and approvals easier to submit and track.
4. Preserve reusable knowledge across departments and personnel changes.
5. Support collaboration and training within the isolated network.
6. Give managers useful operational visibility without unnecessary employee monitoring.
7. Introduce intelligent assistance where its quality and value can be demonstrated.

### 2.2 Scope Boundaries

This report focuses on service ideas, employee experiences, expected business value, governance, and rollout priorities. It does not select a programming framework, database, vendor, deployment topology, or detailed system architecture.

Public product names below describe familiar service concepts only. They are not procurement recommendations or claims that those products can operate offline. Any later product assessment must verify deployment, licensing, dependencies, and maintenance requirements.

## 4. High-Value Services in Detail

### 4.1 My Workplace: The Employee Homepage

An employee opens the portal and sees pending approvals, assigned tasks, upcoming meetings, important notices, and frequently used applications. The homepage should answer: “What needs my attention today?”

Initial features should include a search box, application shortcuts, company news, department contacts, and links to help. Task and approval summaries can be added as systems become ready to supply reliable information.

Personalization should support different departments and job roles. Users should be able to reorder useful shortcuts without hiding mandatory notices. Every displayed task should link directly to its authoritative business record.

**Success evidence:** Employees can locate their applications and important work with fewer steps; active use is sustained after the initial launch.

### 4.2 Enterprise Search

An employee searches for “annual leave policy” and receives the current approved policy, an explanatory FAQ, and the responsible HR contact. A technical employee searches for a system name and finds authorized procedures and known issues.

Results should show the title, source, owner, update date, and useful filters. Current approved guidance should be distinguishable from drafts and superseded documents. Employees should be able to report missing or misleading results.

Permission checks must cover titles, snippets, suggestions, previews, and opened records. Restricting only the final document page is insufficient. Permission changes and deletions must also be reflected in search behavior.

Start with a few maintained sources. Private email, personnel records, and other sensitive sources require separate justification and access rules rather than automatic inclusion.

**Success evidence:** A test set of representative questions produces useful authorized results, and employees can find the correct current information.

### 4.3 Knowledge Base, Q&A, and Expert Finder

The knowledge base stores reviewed procedures; Q&A captures practical questions and answers; the employee directory helps locate people who can assist.

For example, an employee asks how to request a replacement laptop. An expert links the official procedure and explains a common exception. Repeated questions can be consolidated, and useful answers can be proposed for inclusion in the reviewed knowledge base.

Articles need owners, review dates, version history, and clear approval status. Q&A needs moderation, tags, duplicate handling, and correction tools. An accepted answer indicates that the questioner found it helpful; it should not automatically become company policy.

Expert profiles should rely on declared or reviewed skills and relevant work history. Participation should not become a hidden employee performance score, and employees should have a way to correct their profiles.

**Success evidence:** Useful questions receive answers, repeated support questions decline in selected categories, and important articles remain current.

### 4.4 Unified Notifications and Work Queue

Employees should have one place to review relevant messages, approvals, assignments, reminders, and document changes. The underlying business application remains responsible for the actual action and status.

Features should include categories, read/unread status, grouped routine notices, reminder snoozing, quiet periods where appropriate, and direct links. Severity can range from informational to emergency, but business owners must define when each level is valid.

Critical notices may require acknowledgment, a deadline, and escalation. Acknowledgment confirms receipt; it does not prove that a required task is completed. An intranet notice service also does not replace established emergency communication procedures.

**Success evidence:** Important work is easier to locate, notification duplication decreases, and required acknowledgments are traceable.

### 4.5 Forms, Workflow, and Service Requests

Consider an equipment request. An employee completes a form, the manager approves it, IT checks availability, and procurement acts when necessary. The requester can see the current owner and status throughout.

Forms should support required fields, dropdowns, dates, employee/department selectors, attachments, conditional questions, validation, and confirmation. Workflow should support rejection reasons, returned requests, delegation, absence coverage, escalation, cancellation, and history.

Published form and workflow changes should be versioned so an ongoing request has a defined handling rule. Start with a few stable processes before introducing a general graphical workflow designer.

**Success evidence:** Selected requests have fewer incomplete submissions, clearer ownership, and shorter measured completion times.

### 4.6 Document Management and Collaboration

A policy author creates a draft, reviewers comment, an owner approves publication, and employees see the current effective version. Older versions remain available only as permitted and are clearly marked.

Document management should cover ownership, versions, classification, review status, access, retention, and controlled sharing. A drive serves general file exchange; it should not automatically be treated as the authoritative policy repository.

Browser editing and real-time collaboration can be introduced where they solve an actual need. They add operational and compatibility demands and should be piloted with representative document types.

**Success evidence:** Employees identify the correct version, reviewers can track changes, and duplicate authoritative copies decrease.

### 4.7 Learning and Video

New employees should receive a clear learning path containing company orientation, role-specific guidance, and required security training. Managers and training owners should see completion status appropriate to their responsibilities.

Features include courses, videos, captions, assessments, progress, completion records, and searchable transcripts. Timestamped transcript results can take an employee to a relevant section of a long recording. Automatic transcription and chapters require review, particularly for specialist terms and sensitive content.

Completion records alone do not demonstrate understanding. Where appropriate, use assessments or observed task outcomes to evaluate effectiveness.

**Success evidence:** Employees can locate training, mandatory completion is tracked, and onboarding quality improves against an agreed baseline.

### 4.8 Internal AI Assistant

An internal assistant could explain a procedure, summarize an authorized report, draft a status update, or compare two policy versions. Initial use should focus on read-only assistance and employee-reviewed drafts.

Answers about company rules should identify the internal sources and versions used. When evidence is missing or conflicting, the assistant should say so and direct the employee to the responsible owner. Source links improve reviewability but do not guarantee an answer is correct.

The assistant must respect the user's access rights across retrieved content, summaries, history, and exports. Retrieved documents should not be allowed to grant permissions or override service controls. Private deployment by itself does not establish these protections.

Pilot evaluation should use approved questions, known answers, unsupported-question cases, and access-boundary tests. Approval decisions, policy changes, or business record modifications should remain outside the initial scope.

**Success evidence:** Employee-reviewed outputs are useful and accurate enough for the chosen task, unsupported answers are handled properly, and restricted information is not exposed.

## 6. Requirements for an Isolated Intranet

Services must continue operating without public Internet access. Required browser assets, fonts, scripts, styles, help content, and runtime dependencies should be available within the approved environment. External analytics, public CDNs, online authentication, or remote license checks must not silently become operating dependencies.

Software, model, reference-content, and security updates need an approved import and review process. Offline operation does not remove the need for maintenance. Each service should identify what must be updated, who approves imports, and how recovery or rollback works.

Further requirements include:

- Access rules based on employee role, department, and information classification.
- Defined behavior for transfers, departure, temporary assignments, and delegated work.
- Audit records for sensitive access, changes, approvals, and administrative actions.
- Backup and restoration procedures tested against agreed service needs.
- Compatibility with the company’s actual managed devices and browsers.
- Accessible interfaces, readable text, keyboard use, and clear error messages.
- Capacity planning based on peak simultaneous use and workload, rather than assuming all 10,000 employees are active at once.

If multiple isolated environments are involved, their boundaries and permitted exchange mechanisms must be assessed separately. This report does not assume direct connectivity between them.

## 7. Governance and Ownership

| Role | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Executive sponsor | Set priorities, resolve cross-department issues, approve phase funding |
| Product/service owner | Define employee outcomes, backlog, service quality, and adoption |
| Department content owner | Maintain procedures, articles, and department-specific records |
| Process owner | Define approval rules, exceptions, escalation, and service targets |
| IT operations | Maintain availability, support, capacity, backups, and recovery |
| Security and information governance | Define access, classification, audit, retention, and import controls |
| Employee pilot group | Test usability and report real work obstacles |

Every proposed service needs an owner before broad rollout. Content-heavy services need recurring review effort, not only launch funding. Sensitive personal data should have a defined purpose, limited access, and an approved retention rule.

## 8. Recommended Phased Rollout

No implementation dates are assigned here. Staffing, existing services, readiness, and pilot results must determine the schedule.

| Phase | Scope | Exit evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 0 — Discover | Inventory systems, interview departments, measure selected problems, identify owners | Agreed pilot scope, baseline measures, support responsibility |
| 1 — Establish the front door | Portal, application catalog, directory, help center, maintained knowledge, limited search | Useful navigation and reliable authorized information in pilot use |
| 2 — Improve daily transactions | Notifications, forms, selected approvals, tickets, calendar/resource booking | Requests and alerts are reliable; ownership and exceptions are clear |
| 3 — Expand collaboration and knowledge | Q&A, document services, learning, selected project tools | Sustained participation, content review, measurable workflow benefits |
| 4 — Add advanced assistance | Internal AI, video indexing, selected BI/data capabilities | Use-case evaluation passes; operating cost and maintenance are understood |

Monitoring, backups, security controls, and support are prerequisites within each phase. They are not deferred until an employee-facing monitoring or security portal is launched.

A useful first pilot would cover several representative departments and a small set of tasks: locating policy, finding a contact, submitting a service request, and identifying pending work. A single enthusiastic technical team is unlikely to represent all employees.

## 9. Benefits, Measures, and Cost Evaluation

Expected benefits are proposals to test, not established results. Baselines should be collected before setting final targets.

| Outcome | Suggested measure | Interpretation limit |
|---|---|---|
| Faster information discovery | Time and success rate for representative search tasks | Search clicks alone do not prove an answer was useful |
| Better request handling | Completion time, returned submissions, overdue cases | Compare similar request types and complexity |
| Reduced repetitive support | Repeated tickets in selected categories | Check whether employees stopped reporting issues |
| Knowledge reuse | Helpful answers and maintained articles | Raw article counts can reward low-quality content |
| Better onboarding | Time to complete agreed onboarding tasks | Completion does not automatically mean competence |
| Reliable alerts | Duplicate rate and acknowledgment of defined critical notices | Read status does not mean task completion |
| Effective AI assistance | Reviewed accuracy, usefulness, unsupported answers, access tests | Evaluate each use case separately |

For illustration only, if 10,000 employees each saved five minutes on each of 220 working days, the gross annual time equivalent would be:

**10,000 × 5 × 220 ÷ 60 = approximately 183,333 employee-hours.**

This is not a forecast. It assumes all employees benefit every working day. A credible estimate should use measured savings, participating employees, actual adoption, overlap between services, and local working patterns. Saved time does not automatically become a payroll reduction.

The cost assessment should include discovery, development or acquisition, deployment, content preparation, migration, training, support, maintenance, backup capacity, and service ownership. AI, video, and collaborative editing may add substantial compute, storage, and specialist support needs. Their costs should be assessed after use cases are defined.

## 10. Risks and Practical Responses

| Risk | Likely consequence | Practical response |
|---|---|---|
| Too many services launched together | Thin support and inconsistent quality | Use a small pilot and phase gates |
| Stale policies and articles | Employees follow incorrect guidance | Assign owners, review dates, and archival rules |
| Duplicate tools and records | Employees cannot identify the authoritative source | Inventory first; define source ownership |
| Excessive notifications | Important messages are ignored | Group routine notices and govern urgent levels |
| Access leakage through discovery features | Sensitive information becomes visible | Test search, previews, AI, history, and exports |
| Workflow handles only normal cases | Requests become stuck | Include rejection, delegation, cancellation, and escalation |
| Low adoption | Benefits remain unrealized | Test real employee tasks and improve friction points |
| Offline dependencies missed | Service fails after deployment | Validate runtime and update requirements in isolation |
| AI produces unsupported guidance | Employees act on incorrect answers | Limit scope, evaluate quality, show sources, require review |
| No continuing owner or budget | Services deteriorate after launch | Fund recurring operations and content maintenance |

## 11. Recommended Management Decision

Approve discovery and a foundation pilot rather than committing to the entire portfolio. Appoint an executive sponsor and accountable owners, document existing capabilities, and select measurable employee problems before estimating a full program budget.

The first release should provide a useful entry point, trustworthy knowledge, clear contacts, and practical help. Expansion should follow demonstrated demand, permission readiness, and the company’s ability to operate and maintain each service.

## Appendix A — Suggested Discovery Questions

1. Which applications, directories, document stores, and support systems already exist?
2. What information do employees repeatedly struggle to find?
3. Which request types generate the most email follow-up or delays?
4. Who owns current policies, procedures, and training materials?
5. Which departments and employee roles should participate in the pilot?
6. What devices, browsers, languages, and accessibility needs must be supported?
7. Which content may be searched, shared, retained, or used by internal AI?
8. What are the approved procedures for importing updates into the isolated environment?
9. Who will maintain each service after launch, and with what recurring budget?
10. What baseline evidence will demonstrate that the pilot improved work?

## Appendix B — Evidence and Assumptions

This document is a strategic proposal based on the discussion supplied by the requester. It is not an empirical research study or an externally verified market analysis. The stated company size and Internet isolation are supplied premises; current systems, budget, staffing, network boundaries, usage patterns, and readiness remain to be assessed.

Public service names serve only as functional analogies. No external product capabilities, performance comparisons, licensing terms, or availability claims are used to justify the recommendations. Benefits and rollout order are proposed judgments requiring local validation.
